\section{Introduction}

Interactive applications such as games, virtual reality, and other online rendering, demand photorealistic imagery in real time (typically $\geq 60$~FPS).
The standard baking-and-rasterization pipeline meets this by precomputing appearance offline into textures over the mesh, which a fragment shader then samples cheaply at run time~\citep{RealTimeRendering}.


Automating this stage, however, remains hard for the shiny, view-dependent materials that matter most:
the usual baked formats, for instance, lightmaps or low-order Spherical Harmonic (SH) probes, store only smooth, view-independent lighting and miss angle-dependent highlights~\citep{AllFrequency, curveSurfaces, nerfTexture}.
Baking also presupposes a global UV atlas, whose automatic construction remains fragmented and largely manual~\citep{Levy2002LSCM, poranne2017autocuts, li2018optcuts, srinivasan2024nuvo}. Per-face texturing such as Ptex~\citep{ptex} removes the UV atlas, but as a static texture it still cannot capture high-frequency, view-dependent reflection.
The seminal automatic neural baking method is Deferred Neural Rendering (DNR)~\citep{DNR}, which bakes learned neural textures onto a mesh and decodes them at render time.
However, DNR shares the same weakness as classical baking and cannot reproduce view-dependent color.
Our method is a direct, modern successor to it. Like DNR, it is a traditional baking method that produces a baked mesh asset. Unlike DNR, it also captures the sharp, view-dependent appearance that baking has always lacked.

Such appearance has instead been the domain of neural and splat representations. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs)~\citep{NeRFVanilla} and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\citep{3DGS} have revolutionized novel view synthesis,
reaching a level of photorealism that classical baking cannot match by treating the scene as a continuous optimization problem.
Their power, however, rests on a mental model at odds with the one the traditional pipeline assumes.
Mathematically, the baking pipeline treats an object as a \emph{surface}: a closed two-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ that bounds a solid and carries appearance as a field defined on $\mathcal{M}$ itself.
NeRF and 3DGS are objects of a different kind. They represent density-and-color \emph{distribution} over the ambient space $\mathbb{R}^3$ (a continuous field for NeRF, a mixture of anisotropic kernels for 3DGS).
Their domain is the 3-space, not a 2-manifold; nowhere is there a surface, only mass floating in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

This gap is fundamental rather than incidental. Despite a sustained effort to convert splats back into meshes~\citep{sugar, gaussianSurfels},
such methods only ever \emph{approximate} a surface, because a distribution over $\mathbb{R}^3$ is not a manifold:
it has no intrinsic 2D domain and no connected boundary, and no post-processing can recover a structure that was never represented.
2DGS~\citep{2DGS} appears to close the gap by flattening each ellipsoid into a disk---a Gaussian collapsed to a delta across its normal direction.
But this shrinks only the \emph{thickness} of the distribution, not its \emph{category}:
a sum of such primitives scattered through $\mathbb{R}^3$ is still a distribution over the ambient space, not a closed 2-manifold.
Furthermore, forcefully binding such kernels to a fixed triangle face only makes matters worse. The splats become strictly co-planar.
As a result, their depth keys collapse into ties, and the sort order turns ill-defined and unstable across views, producing inconsistent gradients that block convergence.
It is this unfilled step, not any shortcoming of the methods, that keeps these powerful representations from fitting naturally into the traditional pipeline.

Those challenges point to the representation we actually seek: one that keeps the explicit surface geometry of a mesh, so the object stays a true 2D manifold that a rasterizer can consume directly.
It must also inherit the novel-view-synthesis quality of splat and neural representations, together with the differentiable optimization that produces it.
We realize this representation through a novel baking pipeline in which explicit, known geometry provides the structural foundation.
Our method takes three inputs: a triangle mesh, a set of camera poses, and the corresponding reference images.
In our experiments the mesh is an artist-authored synthetic asset, the standard target of baking workflows, and the reference images come from an offline renderer.
From these, we optimize a compact, rasterizer-native appearance representation:
a 2D Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) anchored to the mesh manifold that stores the diffuse color and reflectivity,
a fast Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN)~\citep{FastKAN,KAN} designed to approximate the reflection equation,
and an adaptive, hierarchical neural environment map that partitions the object into near-convex parts to capture self-reflection and occlusion.
At runtime, all three components are evaluated jointly inside a standard fragment shader.



Within this pipeline, we adopt a simple mental model: the appearance at each surface point is a local diffuse color plus a single, gated reflection that covers both far-field and near-field (self-)reflection. These three modules are interchangeable---the pipeline is a \emph{framework} for automatic asset baking, and the realizations above are strong defaults that can be swapped without altering the rest.

In summary, our main contributions are four-fold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Differentiable 2D Asset Baking Pipeline:} We propose a differentiable, sort-free pipeline that bakes appearance directly onto a known mesh by anchoring a strictly 2D appearance field to its faces in barycentric coordinates. The pipeline is a framework rather than a single representation: the surface kernel is interchangeable, and per-face texturing such as Ptex~\citep{ptex} or other 2D kernels could occupy the same slot. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first differentiable method to optimize an element-wise ``texture'' \emph{on} a 2D manifold rather than as a distribution in ambient $\mathbb{R}^3$; it replaces the global $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$ depth sort, together with its co-planarity degeneracy on fixed geometry, with an $\mathcal{O}(K)$ per-fragment local gather that runs natively in a standard rasterizer at a game-ready 60~FPS. As our default kernel we choose a 2D Gaussian mixture for four properties: \emph{continuity}, a smooth field with stable gradients for optimization; \emph{anisotropy}, covariances that align to directional surface detail; \emph{mergeability}, components that prune and combine into a compact, resolution-free representation~\citep{nanogs}; and \emph{convertibility}, a mixture that rasterizes back into a standard texture map for the traditional pipeline.
    \item \textbf{Diffuse via a 2D Gaussian Mixture:} We model the local, view-independent diffuse term with a 2D Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) carrying per-disk albedo and reflectivity on the manifold. The mixture is interchangeable with other surface densities, such as a 2D Beta mixture.
    \item \textbf{Far-Field Reflection via a KAN and Environment Map:} We capture high-frequency specular reflection with a fast Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN) that evaluates the reflection equation against a neural environment map. The decoder is interchangeable, for example a transformer or a precomputed lookup table.
    \item \textbf{Self-Reflection via Hierarchical Convex Environment Maps:} We decompose a non-convex object into near-convex parts, each with its own environment map through an adaptive process. This mechanism largely resolves self-reflection through local environment lookups.\end{itemize}
