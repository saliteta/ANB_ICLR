\section{Experiments}
\vspace{-1mm}
\label{sec:Exp}
\textbf{Setting.} We evaluate on NeRF-Synthetic~\citep{NeRFVanilla} and Shiny-Blender~\citep{refnerf}
in a \emph{fixed-mesh setting}, and on real captures with reconstructed meshes in
Sec.~\ref{sec:real}. In every experiment, ours and every baseline (including the UV-texture baseline)
recover appearance only from the posed images. No method reads the textures of the synthetic
assets. Fixed-mesh baselines get the same ground-truth mesh with geometry frozen:
DNR~\citep{DNR}; a mesh-constrained 2DGS, denoted 2DGS$^{*}$~\citep{2DGS}, initialized with four
million 2D Gaussians uniformly on the surface, with all gradients projected onto the faces;
nvdiffrec~\citep{nvdiffrec}, split-sum inverse rendering with a learned cube-map light, the closest
classical real-time shading model; and RefGS~\citep{refgs} bound to the mesh (Gaussians projected to
their nearest face and aligned to it, keeping its deferred shading and environment map). Fixed-mesh
methods are evaluated at $2\times$ SSAA. As context we also report no-mesh methods
(Ref-NeRF~\citep{refnerf}, 3DGS~\citep{3DGS}, 3iGS~\citep{3igs}, ENVIDR~\citep{envidr},
3DGS-DR~\citep{3dgsDR}) and flexible-mesh methods (GaussianShader~\citep{gaussianShader},
RefGS~\citep{refgs}). These optimize their own geometry, so they are not directly comparable.

\subsection{Ordering Degeneracy}
\label{sec:ordering}
Theorem B (App.~\ref{app:theory}) predicts that a fixed normal offset cannot repair the ordering
obstruction of Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}: every finite offset still flips at some visible view, and the
offset itself receives no learning signal. We confirm this on 2DGS$^{*}$. We displace every disk by a
fixed $\varepsilon$ along the face normal at initialization, with no further normal motion during
training. PSNR stays flat from $\varepsilon{=}10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ and matches the tied
$\varepsilon{=}0$ baseline (Table~\ref{tab:ordering}).

Theorem B4 leaves one view-stable sort: a fixed index order. Table~\ref{tab:fixed_index} isolates the
compositing rule. All three variants are RGB-only, with no environment map or decoder, and share
initialization and training. Fixed-index compositing removes popping, but PSNR \emph{drops}
($27.70\to26.89$\,dB). Here each primitive's color is view-independent, so in depth-sorted 2DGS$^{*}$
the only source of view dependence is reordering: as the view rotates, the front-most primitive at a
fragment changes. On reflective scenes the optimizer exploits this to fake reflections, at the cost
of popping. A fixed order removes the reordering, and with it the only view-dependent mechanism in
this setting, so the rendered appearance becomes strictly view-independent. This is exactly what
Corollary A3 predicts: reordering is the only way past the locality floor, and it is the same
mechanism that causes popping. Our order-free blend is also view-stable and gains $+3.7$\,dB over the
sorted baseline and $+4.5$\,dB over the fixed order on the same mesh. Fixed-index 2DGS$^{*}$ with
per-primitive SH would regain some view dependence, but Theorem A1 bounds it. We therefore move view
dependence out of the primitives and into shared environment maps
(Secs.~\ref{sec:env}--\ref{sec:kan}).

\subsection{Appearance Quality}
\label{sec:quality}
Given the mesh, our appearance field reaches $38.04$~dB PSNR on Shiny-Blender and $33.74$~dB on
NeRF-Synthetic (Table~\ref{tab:main}), the best result among the fixed-mesh methods that share our
input mesh. Split-sum shading with the same mesh (nvdiffrec) trails by $6.3$~dB and loses on every
scene and metric (Table~\ref{tab:nvdiffrec}). The gap is largest on mirror-like \emph{ball} and
\emph{helmet}, where the prefiltered lookup blurs high-frequency detail, and stays large on concave
\emph{toaster}, where one environment map cannot represent self-reflection. Binding a strong recent
splat method to the mesh does not preserve its quality: RefGS reaches $34.80$~dB with its own free
geometry but $30.08$~dB on the mesh. Its large gain from supersampling ($27.31$~dB at $1\times$,
$30.08$~dB at $2\times$) is consistent with the ordering artifacts of Theorem B, which supersampling
partly averages out. The no-mesh and flexible-mesh rows reconstruct their own geometry and are shown
as context, not ranked. Our margin over the best of them (Table~\ref{tab:shiny_perscene}) tracks how
reflective a scene is: $+6.12$~dB on the near-mirror \emph{ball} and $+0.45$~dB on \emph{teapot},
whose highlights are smallest. Qualitatively (Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative}), our renders reproduce
sharp, high-frequency reflections on metallic surfaces that 2DGS$^{*}$ and DNR blur.

\subsection{Real Captures with Reconstructed Geometry}
\label{sec:real}
Synthetic scenes remove geometric error and capture noise. To test the pipeline where both must be
handled, we use the real captured scenes released with Ref-NeRF~\citep{refnerf} (\emph{sedan}; and
\emph{gardenspheres} and \emph{toycar}, originally from SNeRG~\citep{bakingNeRF}). Protocol: (1) we
train RefGS on each scene and export its mesh by TSDF fusion and marching cubes; this mesh carries
real reconstruction errors. (2) We crop the mesh to the central region with the reflective objects.
(3) On this same mesh we train RefGS anchored to the mesh (30K iterations), our method with the mesh
frozen, and our method with the mesh refinement of App.~\ref{app:refinement}. On the same imperfect
mesh, our method beats RefGS on every scene (Table~\ref{tab:real}; $+0.44$~dB mean). Refining the
mesh through our representation adds another $+0.49$~dB, so the pipeline both recovers appearance and
improves the geometry it is baked onto.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} follows the path by which we built the appearance model. The coplanar
2DGS$^{*}$ baseline suffers the depth-fighting of Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}, and a purely diffuse mixture
cannot represent view-dependent reflection, so both collapse on the reflective scenes. Per-primitive
view-dependent color (spherical harmonics in 3DGS, or the anisotropic spherical Gaussians of
Spec-Gaussian~\citep{specGaussian}) is strong for \emph{floating} splats, where overlapping,
depth-ordered primitives compose the appearance. On a single opaque surface that composition is
unavailable: each point takes one order-independent blend. A per-primitive spherical Gaussian with an
MLP decoder therefore only overfits the sparse views ($34.24$~dB), and dropping it in favor of a
single shared environment map changes the result little ($34.37$~dB). The gains come instead from the
adaptive hierarchical maps ($+1.2$~dB over a single global map) and from the KAN decoder
($+2.44$~dB over a parameter-matched MLP). App.~\ref{app:splitsum} replaces the KAN with prefiltered
lookups and with a full split-sum shader inside our pipeline. Prefiltered lookups match our quality
($37.9$~dB) at lower training cost. Split-sum trains about $3.5\times$ faster but loses $4.3$~dB, a
clear quality--speed trade-off.

\textbf{Representation versus a UV texture.} We do not claim that the 2D mixture beats a UV texture
in quality at matched capacity. With both storing diffuse color only and no decoder, environment map
or anti-aliasing, at roughly matched raw parameter counts ($10^6$ disks versus a $1730^2$ texture),
the mixture reaches $27.11$~dB against $26.93$~dB on NeRF-Synthetic at similar frame rates
($98.8$ vs.\ $101.8$ FPS; Table~\ref{tab:uv}). The difference is in capability: the mixture needs no
global parameterization, so resolution can be added, removed or merged locally, and splat tools act
on it directly (App.~\ref{app:uv}).

\subsection{Efficiency and Compaction}
\label{sec:compaction}
The full asset ($10^6$ disks, five environment maps, shader) occupies $48.3$ MB on disk. At
$800{\times}800$ we render at $91.7$ FPS on NeRF-Synthetic and $68.6$ FPS on
Shiny-Blender on an NVIDIA H100 (CUDA 12.8), and at $76.8$ FPS on Shiny-Blender on a consumer RTX
4090 (WSL2, Ubuntu 22.04), with a mean CUDA memory footprint of $1.74$ GB (peak $2.75$ GB), all under
\texttt{torch.compile}. Because each disk is an ordinary splat, we compact the baked asset with
NanoGS~\citep{nanogs}, a training-free Gaussian-splat simplifier, with no change to our
representation. Keeping 50\%, 25\% and 10\% of the disks (Table~\ref{tab:compaction}), mean
PSNR falls by only $2.0$~dB at $10\times$ compaction ($38.04\to36.03$). \emph{Teapot} and
\emph{ball} are essentially unaffected, and the detailed \emph{helmet} loses the most
(App.~\ref{app:compaction}).
