\section{Experiments}
\vspace{-1mm}
\label{sec:Exp}
\textbf{Setting.} We evaluate on NeRF-Synthetic~\citep{NeRFVanilla} and Shiny-Blender~\citep{refnerf}
in a \emph{fixed-mesh setting} (real captures in App.~\ref{app:real}). All methods recover appearance
only from posed images; none reads the assets' textures. Fixed-mesh baselines share our ground-truth
mesh with geometry frozen and are evaluated at $2\times$ SSAA: DNR~\citep{DNR}; 2DGS$^{*}$, a
mesh-constrained 2DGS~\citep{2DGS} with four million disks and gradients projected onto the faces;
nvdiffrec~\citep{nvdiffrec}, split-sum inverse rendering; and RefGS~\citep{refgs} bound to the mesh
(App.~\ref{app:baselines}). No-mesh and flexible-mesh methods optimize their own geometry and are
reported as context only.

\subsection{Ordering Degeneracy}
\label{sec:ordering}
Theorem B (App.~\ref{app:theory}) predicts that a fixed normal offset cannot repair the ordering
obstruction of Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}: every finite offset still flips at some visible view, and the
offset itself receives no learning signal. We confirm this on 2DGS$^{*}$. We displace every disk by a
fixed $\varepsilon$ along the face normal at initialization, with no further normal motion during
training. PSNR stays flat from $\varepsilon{=}10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ and matches the tied
$\varepsilon{=}0$ baseline (Table~\ref{tab:ordering}).

Theorem B4 leaves one view-stable sort, a fixed index order. With RGB-only primitives
(Table~\ref{tab:fixed_index}), it removes popping but \emph{lowers} PSNR ($27.70\to26.89$\,dB):
reordering was the only source of view dependence, which depth-sorted 2DGS$^{*}$ exploits to fake
reflections at the cost of popping (Corollary A3). Our order-free blend is equally view-stable and
gains $+3.7$ and $+4.5$\,dB over the sorted and fixed orders.

\subsection{Appearance Quality}
\label{sec:quality}
Given the mesh, our appearance field reaches $38.04$~dB PSNR on Shiny-Blender and $33.74$~dB on
NeRF-Synthetic (Table~\ref{tab:main}), the best result among the fixed-mesh methods that share our
input mesh. Split-sum shading with the same mesh (nvdiffrec) trails by $6.3$~dB and loses on every
scene and metric (Table~\ref{tab:nvdiffrec}). The gap is largest on mirror-like \emph{ball} and
\emph{helmet}, where the prefiltered lookup blurs high-frequency detail, and stays large on concave
\emph{toaster}, where one environment map cannot represent self-reflection. Bound to the mesh, RefGS
drops from $34.80$~dB (own geometry) to $30.08$~dB, and its large gain from supersampling is
consistent with Theorem B's ordering artifacts (App.~\ref{app:baselines}). The no-mesh and flexible-mesh rows reconstruct their own geometry and are shown
as context, not ranked. Our margin over the best of them (Table~\ref{tab:shiny_perscene}) tracks how
reflective a scene is: $+6.12$~dB on the near-mirror \emph{ball} and $+0.45$~dB on \emph{teapot},
whose highlights are smallest. Qualitatively (Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative}), our renders reproduce
sharp, high-frequency reflections on metallic surfaces that 2DGS$^{*}$ and DNR blur.
On real captures (Ref-NeRF's real scenes), using an imperfect mesh reconstructed by RefGS, we beat
RefGS bound to that same mesh on every scene ($24.12$ vs.\ $23.68$~dB). Refining the mesh through our
representation raises this to $24.61$~dB (App.~\ref{app:real}).

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} follows the path by which we built the appearance model. The coplanar
2DGS$^{*}$ baseline suffers the depth-fighting of Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}, and a purely diffuse mixture
cannot represent view-dependent reflection, so both collapse on the reflective scenes. Per-primitive
view-dependent color (spherical harmonics in 3DGS, or the anisotropic spherical Gaussians of
Spec-Gaussian~\citep{specGaussian}) is strong for \emph{floating} splats, where overlapping,
depth-ordered primitives compose the appearance. On a single opaque surface that composition is
unavailable: each point takes one order-independent blend. A per-primitive spherical Gaussian with an
MLP decoder therefore only overfits the sparse views ($34.24$~dB), and dropping it in favor of a
single shared environment map changes the result little ($34.37$~dB). The gains come instead from the
adaptive hierarchical maps ($+1.2$~dB over a single global map) and from the KAN decoder
($+2.44$~dB over a parameter-matched MLP). Prefiltered lookups in place of the sharp fetch match our quality, and a full split-sum shader
trains $3.5\times$ faster but loses $4.3$~dB (App.~\ref{app:splitsum}).

At matched capacity with diffuse color only, the 2D mixture and a UV texture are on par in quality
and speed ($27.11$ vs.\ $26.93$~dB; App.~\ref{app:uv}); the mixture's advantage is capability, not
quality.

\subsection{Efficiency and Compaction}
\label{sec:compaction}
The full asset ($10^6$ disks, five maps, shader) takes $48.3$ MB and renders at
$800{\times}800$ at $91.7$ / $68.6$ FPS (NeRF-Synthetic / Shiny-Blender) on an H100 and $76.8$ FPS on
Shiny-Blender on an RTX 4090 (mean memory $1.74$ GB). Since each disk is an ordinary splat,
NanoGS~\citep{nanogs} compacts the asset with no change to our pipeline: at $10\times$ compaction
PSNR falls by only $2.0$~dB (Table~\ref{tab:compaction}, App.~\ref{app:compaction}).
