\subsection{Mesh Refinement Algorithm}
\label{app:refinement}
We propose a mesh refinement algorithm, which we refer to as \textbf{mesh fine-tuning}. Mesh fine-tuning reads, from ANB representation, the direction each disk \emph{would} move in the ambient Euclidian space to reduce photometric loss, and keeps only the normal-direction component that neighboring disks agree on. We then propagate this movement back onto the vertices as a per-vertex displacement and update the mesh. The core process is visualized in Figure \ref{fig:mesh_finetune}.

To assess mesh fine-tuning, we first warm-up our appearance model on the extracted mesh from Ref-GS for 625 optimizer steps and then \emph{freeze} the environment map and KAN shader. Thus, any remaining error can be solely attributed to geometric problems with the mesh. We then run mesh fine-tuning and compare the resulting mesh with and without our mesh fine-tuning in Figure \ref{fig:mesh_qual} against the ground truth Blender model.

Overall, we find that our proposed mesh fine-tuning is able to refine the extracted mesh and reduce its normal angular error when compared to the ground truth normal across all 6 scenes in ShinyBlender. We report Mean Average Error (MAE) of per-vertex normals and mesh face normals in Table \ref{tab:mesh_normal}. The algorithm also improves accuracy on 5/6 scenes and Chamfer distance (CD) on 4/6, with \emph{car} unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:refinement_chamfer}). We also assess the effect of any appearance regression of our algorithm as measured by PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPs in Table \ref{tab:mesh_appearance}.

\paragraph{Mesh Fine-tuning Algorithm.}
We outline some details of our proposed mesh fine-tuning algorithm given our ANB representation here. Our algorithm alternates between a geometry step (the flow is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:mesh_finetune} and an appearance step outlined in Eq. \ref{eq:appearance}. We now detail aspects of the geometry step.

% Each cycle of the algorithm alternates a geometry stage with an appearance stage.  During the geometry stage, we have

% \paragraph{setup} We have a map from disk space to vertex space (i.e. $B: V \rightarrow E$). 

\textbf{Detach and Read Gradient.} We \emph{temporarily} release disk $i$ from the
surface, treating its center $\mathbf{x}_i$ as a free variable in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ rather than a point constrained to the mesh. This lets the
disk respond to motion along the surface normal, and we read off the direction that
would most reduce the photometric loss. It tells us where the disk would go
\emph{if} it could leave the surface---ignoring, for a moment, that the disk
is pinned to the mesh and cannot move on its own. The freed center point is read
for its gradient and then discarded.

Concretely, we project disk centers $\mathbf{X}$ and detach the projected point from the computation graph for a single
backward pass. Then, we run 1 forward optimization step, evaluating the disk in 2D-GS ray-splat space \cite{2DGS}, rather than in-place. Draw training images $I^{train}$ and running photometric loss $\mathcal{L}_{app}$, we have

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{V},
  \qquad
  \hat{\mathbf{X}} \leftarrow \mathrm{sg}\!\left[\mathbf{X}\right],
  \qquad
  \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}}
  = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{app}\!\left(
      raysplat(\hat{\mathbf{X}}),\, I^{train}
    \right)}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{X}}},
\end{equation}

% where $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$ holds the barycentric weights,
% $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times 3}$ the mesh vertex positions, and

where
$\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ is the stop-gradient operator. Thus $\hat{\mathbf{X}}$ carries no dependence on $\mathbf{V}$ and
is an unconstrained point set in $\mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$, and the mesh is still fixed at this stage. $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$ is the off-surface
descent direction we probe for. % and not just autograd %

\textbf{Vertex Gather}
Next, we transfer the freed gradient back into the vertex space, distributing each
disk's requested displacement onto the vertices that support it, weighted by each
vertex's influence on that disk:
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pullback-app}
  \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}}
  = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{1}\right),
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times 3}$.
Note that we intentionally design the gradient flow from the disk field to vertices rather than directly to the vertices.

\textbf{Translating disk movement to vertex displacement}
To turn $\mathbf{G_V}$ into a vertex displacement $\Delta$, we keep only the part of the update the photometric proxy can be trusted to have determined. 

We condition the raw displacement with three operators, applied right to left:
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditioning}
  \mathcal{T} \;=\; \mathcal{C}_{\delta}\,\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}},
\end{equation}
%
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Normal restriction} ($\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{n}}$). We keep only the
    component along the vertex normal $\mathbf{{n}_{V}}$ since tangential motion reparameterizes
    the surface without changing its shape.
  \item \textbf{Band-limiting} ($\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}$). We smooth to the disk
    radius $\sigma$ by damped umbrella diffusion, since finer detail cannot be
    represented by the appearance field and therefore cannot be verified against
    the images TODO: cite.
  \item \textbf{Displacement bound} ($\mathcal{C}_{\delta}$). We cap each
    vertex's displacement, so that no single cycle moves a vertex further than a
    later one can undo.
\end{itemize}

Now, the vertex gradient combines the photometric term with the
normal-consistency term, and $\mathcal{T}$ acts on the step the Adam optimizer
realizes rather than on the gradient itself:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:step}
  \Delta \;=\; \mathcal{T}\Big(
    \mathrm{Adam}_{\mathbf{V}}\big(\mathbf{V},\;
      \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{nrm}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{nrm}}
    \big) - \mathbf{V}
  \Big),
\end{equation}
where $Adam$ is the Adam optimizer (cite) and $\lambda_{norm}$ is a hyper-parameter for how much to weigh the normal component of the disk movement.

\textbf{Analysis.}
Qualitatively, mesh fine-tuning improves the refined surface in Column 3 of Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_qual}, particularly in the view-dependent regions where the coarse extraction struggles. We also highlight the ability of mesh fine-tuning to remove a ringing artifact on \emph{teapot} and \emph{helmet} in Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_ringing}. The rings are marching-cubes terraces at the TSDF voxel spacing that survive simplification; they barely move the surface but tilt its face normals. Mesh fine-tuning targets exactly this: its updates are restricted to the normal direction, and the normal-consistency term pulls each face toward the smooth, cross-view shading normal. Consistent with this, median face-normal error falls by $61.8\%$ on \emph{helmet} and $53.4\%$ on \emph{teapot} (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_normal}) while CD changes by under $5\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:refinement_chamfer}).

% $5.58^\circ$ to $1.19^\circ$.

Normal error improves on all six scenes, with mean
MAE falling, $6.31^\circ \rightarrow 5.41^\circ$ and yielding ($+14.2\%$) improvement across scenes. 
Chamfer accuracy corroborates this on five of six scenes
(Table~\ref{tab:refinement_chamfer}), the exception being \textit{teapot}, whose input
mesh is already the second most accurate in the set and therefore has little
geometric slack remaining.
Crucially, these gains are not paid for in appearance: PSNR improves on five of
six scenes for a mean of $0.45$\,dB (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_appearance}), so the
refinement is correcting geometry rather than trading image quality for it.

In our experiment, we highlight an extension of our representation to conduct mesh refinement \emph{without} altering connectivity. Because barycentric anchoring fixes topology, our proposed mesh refinement method can \emph{adjust} existing surface but \emph{cannot recover} surface that is absent (i.e. large holes). We leave extensions such as coupling the vertex update to remeshing, topology-aware extraction stage, and more detailed comparisons against SOTA mesh refinement methods as future work.
