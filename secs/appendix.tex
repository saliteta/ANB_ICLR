\subsection{Theoretical Analysis: Ordering and Locality}
\label{app:theory}
This section formalizes the two obstructions motivating our representation (Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}):
coplanar primitives admit no view-stable, geometrically meaningful compositing order
(\emph{ordering}, Theorem B), and per-primitive view-dependent color models are band-limited at
every fragment regardless of primitive count (\emph{locality}, Theorem A1). We then state
falsifiable predictions of both and map them to evidence already reported in the paper.

\paragraph{Setup.} Fix a fragment $\mathbf{x}$ on a locally planar patch with unit normal
$\mathbf{n}$. Primitives $i=1,\dots,K$ overlap $\mathbf{x}$ with opacity $\alpha_i\in(0,1]$ and
view-dependent color $c_i(\mathbf{v})$, continuous in $\mathbf{v}$. A renderer picks an order $\pi$
and composites
\begin{equation}
C_\pi(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_i w_i^\pi c_i(\mathbf{v}), \qquad
w^\pi_{\pi(k)} = \alpha_{\pi(k)}\prod_{l<k}\big(1-\alpha_{\pi(l)}\big).
\label{eq:theory-composite}
\end{equation}
3DGS and 2DGS sort by view-space center depth $z_i = (\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c})\cdot \mathbf{f}$,
with camera center $\mathbf{c}$ and optical axis $\mathbf{f}$; this is the order we analyze, so the
analysis is exact rather than an orthographic approximation. The mirror target is
$L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}) = E(\boldsymbol{\omega}_r)$ with
$\boldsymbol{\omega}_r = 2(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathbf{n}-\mathbf{v}$ and environment $E$.

\paragraph{Lemma 1 (Coverage is order-invariant).} For every order $\pi$,
$\sum_i w_i^\pi = 1-\prod_i(1-\alpha_i) =: A$. With $T_k=\prod_{l<k}(1-\alpha_{\pi(l)})$,
$w_{\pi(k)}=T_k-T_{k+1}$ telescopes to $T_1-T_{K+1}$, a commutative product independent of $\pi$.

\paragraph{Lemma 2 (Geometry of order flips).} Coplanar centers satisfy
$(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)\perp\mathbf{n}$, so their depth order flips exactly when
$(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)\cdot\mathbf{f}=0$, a condition on the optical axis $\mathbf{f}$ alone,
independent of camera position. On the sphere of possible axes the flip set for each pair is a
great circle through $\pm\mathbf{n}$; taking $\mathbf{n}$ as the pole, these are meridians.
Consequently: pure camera translation never flips a pair; azimuthal rotation about $\mathbf{n}$
crosses every meridian; tilting $\mathbf{f}$ toward or away from $\mathbf{n}$ at fixed azimuth
crosses none unless it passes face-on ($\mathbf{f}\parallel\pm\mathbf{n}$), which flips every pair
at once. The flip rate under rotation scales as $1/\sin\theta$ for $\theta$ the angle between
$\mathbf{f}$ and $\mathbf{n}$: flips are dense face-on and sparse at grazing angles.

\paragraph{Lemma 3 (Exact jump at a flip).} Swapping an adjacent pair $(i,j)$ with prefix
transmittance $T$ changes the composite by
$C_\pi - C_{\pi'} = T\alpha_i\alpha_j\big(c_i(\mathbf{v})-c_j(\mathbf{v})\big)$; more generally, by
Lemma~1, $C_\pi-C_{\pi'}=\sum_i(w_i^\pi-w_i^{\pi'})(c_i-\bar c)$ for any $\bar c$. Every jump is
therefore bounded by $2A$ times the color dispersion of the overlapping primitives, and is zero
exactly when the overlapping colors agree at that view.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Diffuse appearance makes flips harmless).} If
$L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})=L_0$ is view-independent, setting $c_i\equiv L_0/A$ fits $\mathbf{x}$
exactly under every order, so all jumps vanish. With a spatially varying albedo at primitive
spacing $\delta$, the residual jump is at most $T\alpha_i\alpha_j|a(\mathbf{x}_i)-a(\mathbf{x}_j)|
\lesssim \delta|\nabla a|$: a fixed, view-independent blur that shrinks under densification, not
popping.

\paragraph{Proposition 2 (View dependence lower-bounds the jump cost).} Let $p_i,p_j$ be points
each covered only by their own primitive (rendering $\alpha c_i$, $\alpha c_j$ there), and let $\eta$
bound the rendering error at $p_i$, $p_j$, and the two one-sided renders at $\mathbf{x}$ near a
flip view $\mathbf{v}^*$. Then for any continuous colors,
\begin{equation}
\eta \;\ge\; \frac{T\alpha}{2(1+T\alpha)}\,\big|L(p_i,\mathbf{v}^*)-L(p_j,\mathbf{v}^*)\big|.
\label{eq:jump-lb}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof sketch.} The jump is $J=T\alpha(\alpha c_i-\alpha c_j)$ (Lemma~3); the private-point
errors give $|\alpha c_i-\alpha c_j|\ge|\Delta L|-2\eta$; continuity of $L(\mathbf{x},\cdot)$ at
$\mathbf{v}^*$ forces $|J|\le 2\eta$; combining and rearranging gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:jump-lb}. To first
order $|\Delta L|\approx \delta|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\omega}} E|(2\kappa+1/d)$ for a mirror of
curvature $\kappa$ at distance $d$ (the diffuse analogue is $\delta|\nabla a|$), so the floor decays
with surface roughness and vanishes in the diffuse limit, but for sharp environments it approaches
the full environment contrast independent of $\delta$: no amount of densification removes it.

\paragraph{Proposition 3 (Gradient splitting).} As $\alpha\to1$, back primitives receive vanishing
gradient, so primitive $i$ is fit only from the (Lemma-2-determined) azimuthal wedge of training
views in which it is frontmost. A view-dependent color with $m=(\ell+1)^2$ spherical-harmonic
coefficients needs at least $m$ such views to be identified; with fewer, the unconstrained component
freezes at initialization yet is still rendered at test time. A diffuse color ($m=1$) needs only
one.

\paragraph{Lemma A0 (Reflection is a rotation).} $\boldsymbol{\omega}_r=R_\mathbf{n}\mathbf{v}$ with
$R_\mathbf{n}=2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^\top-I$, a rotation by $\pi$ about $\mathbf{n}$ (eigenvalues
$(1,-1,-1)$, determinant $+1$). It maps the visible hemisphere $H_\mathbf{n}$ onto itself and
preserves $P_\ell$, the space of spherical harmonics of degree $\le\ell$.

\paragraph{Theorem A1 (Band limit independent of primitive count).} If every $c_i\in P_\ell$ and
the blend weights at $\mathbf{x}$ are view-independent (a fixed order or a partition of unity),
then for \emph{any} number, position, or weighting of primitives,
\begin{equation}
\|C(\mathbf{x},\cdot)-L(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\|_{L^2(H_\mathbf{n})} \;\ge\;
\tau_\ell(E,\mathbf{n}) := \min_{p\in P_\ell}\|E-p\|_{L^2(H_\mathbf{n})}.
\label{eq:band-limit}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof.} $C(\mathbf{x},\cdot)=\sum_i w_i(\mathbf{x})c_i\in P_\ell$ since $P_\ell$ is a linear
space; by Lemma~A0, $L(\mathbf{x},\cdot)=E\circ R_\mathbf{n}$, and composing with the rotation
$R_\mathbf{n}$ preserves both $P_\ell$ and the $L^2(H_\mathbf{n})$ norm, so the distance from
$L(\mathbf{x},\cdot)$ to $P_\ell$ is exactly $\tau_\ell(E,\mathbf{n})$. $\blacksquare$ The bound is
strictly positive whenever $E$ has edges or sharp lights (on the full sphere it is the SH tail
energy $\sum_{l>\ell}|E_l|^2$), and it does not shrink as $K\to\infty$: \emph{adding primitives
never adds angular bandwidth at a fragment.} The same argument with $P_\ell$ replaced by
$KM$-lobe spherical-Gaussian mixtures gives the analogous floor for SG models.

\paragraph{Corollary A2 (Identifiability caps the usable degree).} Each primitive observes the
fragment's function at most once per training view (Prop.~3), so identifying its $(\ell+1)^2$
coefficients needs $N_v\ge(\ell+1)^2$ views, i.e.\ $\ell_{\max}=\lfloor\sqrt{N_v}\rfloor-1$ and a
generalization floor of $\tau_{\ell_{\max}}(E,\mathbf{n})$. With $100$ training views, $\ell\le9$ at
best, before gradient splitting reduces it further, the mechanism behind the per-primitive
spherical-Gaussian overfitting in Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Corollary A3 (Locality and ordering degenerate together).} In free-floating splatting,
view-dependent selection among overlapping splats is the only mechanism that could exceed the
Theorem-A1 floor. On a plane the order is independent of elevation (Lemma~2), so along any meridian
the rendered function is a single fixed blend in $P_\ell$ and Theorem~A1 applies there; reordering
can only add azimuthal discontinuities (Prop.~2's jumps), never elevation detail. The one escape
from locality is exactly the mechanism that produces popping.

\paragraph{Proposition A4 (A shared environment map removes the floor).} Factor appearance as
$C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})=g\big(a(\mathbf{x}),m(\mathbf{x}),\hat E(R_\mathbf{n}\mathbf{v})\big)$
with per-element, view-independent $(a_i,m_i)$ and a single shared, high-capacity $\hat E$ (our
environment map, Sec.~\ref{sec:env}). Then: (i) error is governed by $|E-\hat E|$, independent of
per-element capacity; (ii) parameters drop from $N(\ell+1)^2$ to $(\ell+1)^2+O(N)$; (iii)
$\hat E$ is constrained by every reflective pixel of every training view, so Corollary~A2's cap
disappears; (iv) merging or pruning elements acts only on the scalar attributes $(a_i,m_i)$, so
elements stay individually addressable and mergeable (App.~\ref{app:compaction}). Per-part maps
(our convex decomposition, Sec.~\ref{sec:env}) make $\hat E$ depend on the part and preserve all
four properties.

\paragraph{Theorem B: no cheap fix to the ordering. Definition.} A fix is \emph{cheap} if it keeps
sort-based compositing with key $k_i=z_i+g_i$, a static per-primitive perturbation of the depth; a
normal offset $\varepsilon_i$ is the canonical example (Sec.~\ref{sec:ordering}).

\paragraph{B1 (Flips are unavoidable).} The pair order is the sign of
$(\Delta\mathbf{x}+\Delta\varepsilon\,\mathbf{n})\cdot\mathbf{f}$, whose flip set is the great
circle normal to $\mathbf{m}=\Delta\mathbf{x}+\Delta\varepsilon\,\mathbf{n}$. If
$\Delta\mathbf{x}\ne0$ then $\mathbf{m}\not\parallel\mathbf{n}$, and every great circle but the
equator enters the visible hemisphere: every pair with distinct centers flips at some visible view,
for any finite offset.

\paragraph{B2 (Suppressing flips costs thickness).} The flip circle's maximum elevation $\phi_{\max}$ above the surface satisfies
$\sin\phi_{\max}=\max(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{f})=|\Delta\mathbf{x}|/\sqrt{|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^2+\Delta\varepsilon^2}$.
Keeping every flip below elevation $\phi$ needs $|\Delta\varepsilon|\ge|\Delta\mathbf{x}|\cot\phi$
for every overlapping pair, which for the widest pair (lateral distance $D$) requires offsets
spanning at least $D\cot\phi$ ($\approx5.7D$ at $\phi=10^\circ$) and diverges as $\phi\to0$:
flip-free rendering to grazing angles needs a layer thicker than the cluster is wide, a volume, not
a surface.

\paragraph{B3 (Offsets are not learnable).} The key affects $C$ only through the discrete order,
and a small normal offset barely changes the projected footprint, so $\partial C/\partial
\varepsilon_i=0$ almost everywhere and $\varepsilon$ freezes at initialization. At our
$\sim\!4$M-primitive spacing ($|\Delta\mathbf{x}|\sim10^{-3}$), offsets $\varepsilon\le10^{-3}$
leave flips reaching $45^\circ$ or more, predicting the flat PSNR of Sec.~\ref{sec:ordering}'s
$\varepsilon$ sweep, which we confirm empirically (Table~\ref{tab:ordering}).

\paragraph{B4 (The only flip-free sort is geometry-free).} A view-stable order must be independent
of depth, either the per-pixel tie-break or the $\varepsilon\to\infty$ limit, and is then a fixed
index order that encodes no geometry.

\paragraph{Falsifiable predictions and evidence.} Beyond the $\varepsilon$ sweep (B2--B3), Lemma~2
predicts that, with 2DGS$^{*}$ anchored to the mesh, pure camera translation causes no reordering
and hence no flicker, while camera rotation reorders with flicker concentrated where the surface
faces the camera ($\propto1/\sin\theta$) and weaker toward silhouettes; under pure rotation about
the camera center every surface point is seen along an unchanged ray, so any flicker is a sorting
artifact by construction. \textbf{We provide qualitative video evidence for this prediction} on
\emph{ball}-like scenes (supplementary video: strong face-on flicker under rotation, none under
translation). This is a qualitative confirmation, not a quantitative one; a quantitative test via
warp-compensated residuals and flip-count heatmaps is left to future work. Table~\ref{tab:theory_evidence} maps the remaining
results already reported in the paper to the claim each one supports.

\paragraph{Scope of the theory.} The argument shows locality (Theorem~A1), not ordering, carries most
of the quality gap: fixed-index compositing survives Theorem~B (it is view-stable, so Prop.~2's
jump bound does not apply) but is still defeated by Theorem~A1 when colors are per-primitive. We
have not run the isolating experiment (2DGS$^{*}$ with fixed-index order plus our environment map
and KAN) that would confirm this directly, nor proven that the order-free partition-of-unity blend
is optimal rather than merely sufficient; we leave both to future work. The per-primitive blur that
Prop.~1 allows ($O(\delta|\nabla a|)$) is continuous and shrinks under densification, unlike the
popping Theorem~B describes.

\subsection{Ablation Details}

Here, we present detailed qualitative and quantitative results from our ablation study comparing our approach against closely related baselines. We first evaluated replacing the KAN with a standard MLP. Additionally, we experimented with incorporating material tokens or features, similar to scaffolded Gaussian Splats; however, the performance was inferior to that of a straightforward MLP. We also attempted to use spherical Gaussians for the view-dependent representation, but this approach yielded suboptimal results. \subsection{Mesh Refinement Through the Anchoring}
\label{app:refinement}
Although our main experiments assume a fixed mesh setting where we let a ground truth mesh drive appearance (i.e. vertices don't move), we explore the capability of ANB's representation in refining an \emph{extracted mesh}. We evaluate this by refining an extracted mesh from a splatting representation (RefGS) and comparing its inferred geometry with a withheld ground truth mesh. 

To form this mesh, we first extract the surface parameterized by RefGS~\citep{refgs}'s Gaussians using TSDF fusion \citep{tsdf} and then extract a mesh using
Marching Cubes~\citep{marchingcubes}. We note that the input mesh carries two normals. The \emph{face normal} $\mathbf{n}_f$ is the geometric normal of each triangle, computed from its current vertex positions; it is also the normal of every disk anchored to that face. The \emph{vertex normal} $\mathbf{n}_v$ is a per-vertex attribute obtained by averaging RefGS's rendered normal maps over the training views; it is interpolated across each face as ANB's shading normal and is optimized along with the mesh during refinement. Both normals, as well as the vertex positions, may differ from the ground truth. We use the agreement of the two normals as a \emph{normal}-consistency prior, which penalizes misalignment between each face normal and the normals of its vertices while keeping $\mathbf{n}_v$ close to its RefGS initialization.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Geometry is scored against the ground-truth Blender models. Normals are
scored by mean angular error (MAE, in degrees) against the ground-truth normal maps of 200 test
views, averaging the unsigned angle over pixels covered by both the mesh and the ground-truth mask.
We report it for the face normals ($\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathbf{n}_f}$), which measure the refined
geometry itself, and for the interpolated vertex normals ($\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathbf{n}_v}$), which are
what ANB shades with. To assess the quality of the vertex
positions of the refined mesh, we use the accuracy term (Acc.) of the Chamfer distance~\citep{dtu}, the mean
distance from points on the refined mesh to the nearest ground-truth surface visible from the test
cameras (lower is better). Following Ref-NeuS~\citep{ref-neus}, we report accuracy rather than
completeness, which is dominated by regions missing from the input mesh that a fixed-connectivity
refinement cannot fill.

Overall, we find ANB is able to refine the input mesh, reducing normal
error and improving accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:refine_improve}). Table~\ref{tab:refine_decomp} shows that the normal-consistency prior is necessary: the photometric
loss alone degrades the face normals ($\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathbf{n}_f}$: $7.20^\circ \rightarrow 18.58^\circ$), while with the normal term alone
no photometric gradient reaches the vertices and the surface moves away from the ground truth
(Acc.: $5.83 \rightarrow 6.18$); only the two together
improve on the extracted mesh on all three metrics.
Refinement reduces $\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathbf{n}_f}$ by $18.3\%$ ($7.20^\circ \rightarrow 5.88^\circ$) and
$\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathbf{n}_v}$ by $14.2\%$ ($6.31^\circ \rightarrow 5.41^\circ$), and improves accuracy by $5.8\%$.

We highlight the effect
of refinement on high-frequency surface artifacts left by the extraction in Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_ringing}: the fine noise on
\emph{teapot} and the concentric marching-cubes ringing on \emph{helmet} are both smoothed away. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_qual} shows the extracted, warmed-up, and refined meshes alongside the ground truth for held-out views of \emph{teapot}, \emph{helmet}, and \emph{toaster}. Because barycentric anchoring fixes topology, our proposed mesh refinement method can \emph{adjust} existing surface but \emph{cannot recover} surface that is absent (i.e. large holes). We leave extensions such as remeshing and more detailed comparisons against SOTA mesh refinement methods as future work.

\subsection{Element-wise Compaction}
\label{app:compaction}
Because each disk is anchored independently in barycentric coordinates and carries its own albedo
and reflectivity (Sec.~\ref{sec:gmm}), it is an ordinary Gaussian splat rather than a representation-specific
primitive. This lets us apply NanoGS~\citep{nanogs}, a training-free splat simplifier developed for
free-floating 3D Gaussian Splatting, directly to our on-manifold disks with no modification: we run
it once on the trained asset to merge and prune disks down to a target fraction, with no further
optimization. Table~\ref{tab:compaction_perscene} details the per-scene results summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:compaction}. Degradation is graceful and uneven across scenes: \emph{teapot} and
\emph{ball} are essentially unaffected down to $10\%$ of the disks, while \emph{helmet}, the most
geometrically detailed scene, loses the most in PSNR and LPIPS. This is the behavior we would expect
from an asset built out of individually addressable, mergeable elements, and it is the property a
baking pipeline needs for automatic level-of-detail generation, the splat analogue of a mipmap
chain, without a bespoke LOD method.
