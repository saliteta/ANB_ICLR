\subsection{Ablation Details}

Here, we present detailed qualitative and quantitative results from our ablation study comparing our approach against closely related baselines. We first evaluated replacing the KAN with a standard MLP. Additionally, we experimented with incorporating material tokens or features, similar to scaffolded Gaussian Splats; however, the performance was inferior to that of a straightforward MLP. We also attempted to use spherical Gaussians for the view-dependent representation, but this approach yielded suboptimal results. These quantitative comparisons are summarized in Tab.~\ref{tab:combined-ablation-baselines}.

Furthermore, we provide qualitative visualizations that decompose the outputs of our method. Specifically, our rendering function consists of three components: multiple environment maps for self-reflection, a KAN for specular reflection, and a GMM for diffuse lighting. We visualize the distinct contributions of each of these lighting components. To isolate the effects of the 2D GMM, Fig.~\ref{fig:abl_qualitative} compares our method against a 2DGS baseline exhibiting depth-fighting (with spherical harmonics set to zero). Finally, Tab.~\ref{tab:shiny_perscene} details quantitative comparison against state-of-the-art methods on the Shiny-Blender dataset.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement Algorithm}
\label{app:refinement}
Sec.~\ref{sec:refinement} shows that appearance gradients reach the mesh through the linear anchoring $\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}$. Here we turn that observation into a simple procedure, which we call \textbf{mesh fine-tuning}. We intend it as a demonstration of this property of the representation rather than as a competitive mesh-refinement method. Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_finetune} illustrates one geometry step.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/mesh_refinement.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Mesh fine-tuning, one geometry step} (Eq.~\ref{eq:cycle}), shown for one
    disk $i$ on face $f_i$ with corners $\mathbf{v}_0,\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2$.
    (a) The disk center is detached into a free copy $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$. This copy is evaluated in
    ray-splat form (Sec.~\ref{sec:refinement}) over a small batch of training views, and we read its
    off-surface gradient $\mathbf{g}_i=\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{app}}/\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$.
    (b) The gradient is split over the face's corners by the barycentric weights that reconstruct
    the disk, $\mathbf{g}_{j\leftarrow i}=B_{ij}\,\mathbf{g}_i$.
    (c) Each vertex averages the shares it receives from every disk on its incident faces,
    $\mathbf{g}_{\mathbf{v}_j}=d_j^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf{g}_{j\leftarrow i}$ with $d_j=\sum_i B_{ij}$.
    (d) The result is conditioned into a displacement $\Delta_j$ and committed,
    $\mathbf{v}_j\leftarrow\mathbf{v}_j+\Delta_j$, and the disk centers are re-anchored. The disk
    itself never leaves the surface; it only serves as a probe.}
    \label{fig:mesh_finetune}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mesh Fine-tuning Algorithm.}
Each cycle is a geometry step (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_finetune}) followed by an appearance step. The
geometry step reads the disk gradient, pulls it back to the vertices (Eq.~\eqref{eq:pullback}), and
turns it into a vertex step:
%
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{app}}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{X}}}\bigg|_{\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}},
\qquad
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}},
\\
\Delta^{\text{Adam}} = \mathrm{Adam}_{\mathbf{V}}\big(\mathbf{V},\,
  \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}} + \lambda_{\text{nrm}}\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}R_{\text{nrm}}\big) - \mathbf{V},
\qquad
\mathbf{V} \leftarrow \mathbf{V} + \mathcal{T}\big(\Delta^{\text{Adam}}\big).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:cycle}
\end{equation}
%
Here $\mathrm{Adam}_{\mathbf{V}}(\mathbf{V},\mathbf{G})$ is the position that one step of
Adam~\citep{adam} would reach from $\mathbf{V}$ given gradient $\mathbf{G}$, so
$\Delta^{\text{Adam}}$ is the step Adam would actually take. $R_{\text{nrm}}=\sum_f a_f\,\big(1-\cos(\mathbf{n}_f,\bar{\mathbf{n}}_f)\big)\big/\sum_f a_f$
is an area-weighted ($a_f$) normal-consistency term between each face's geometric normal
$\mathbf{n}_f$ and its shading normal $\bar{\mathbf{n}}_f$, the normalized mean of the face's three
vertex shading normals, which are baked from the input and held fixed. $\lambda_{\text{nrm}}$ is a
relative weight: $\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}R_{\text{nrm}}$ is rescaled once, on the first cycle, to
$\lambda_{\text{nrm}}$ times the norm of $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{V}}$.
$\mathcal{T}$ conditions the step: it restricts it to the vertex-normal direction, band-limits it to
the disk radius $\sigma$~\citep{TODO-umbrella-smoothing}, and bounds its per-vertex magnitude. In
the appearance step, $\mathbf{V}$ is fixed and the disk attributes are optimized on
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{app}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}). We repeat the cycle $n$ times.

\textbf{The optimizer step as the unit.} What we control is how the vertices move, and that is
decided by $\Delta^{\text{Adam}}$, not by the gradient. Adam rescales each world axis by its own
running statistics. Everything upstream of it, from the disk probe through the pullback, therefore
fixes only a direction and the relative weight of the photometric and normal-consistency terms. It
does not fix the size of the motion. This is also why we average rather than sum in the pullback:
$\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}}$ grows with local disk count, and $\mathbf{D}^{-1}$ keeps
the photometric term's weight against $\lambda_{\text{nrm}}$ the same everywhere on the mesh. By the
same argument, any constraint on how the mesh may move must be imposed on the step itself. A
gradient projected onto $\mathbf{n}_{v_j}$ generally does not produce a step along
$\mathbf{n}_{v_j}$, and a capped gradient does not bound the resulting displacement. $\mathcal{T}$
is therefore applied to $\Delta^{\text{Adam}}$.

\paragraph{Implementation Details}

To assess mesh fine-tuning, we start from the Ref-GS mesh, extracted by TSDF fusion and marching cubes and then simplified and hole-filled (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_qual}, columns 1--2). We first warm up our appearance model on this mesh for 625 optimizer steps and then \emph{freeze} the environment map and KAN shader. Thus, any remaining error can be solely attributed to geometric problems with the mesh. We then run mesh fine-tuning and compare the resulting mesh with and without our mesh fine-tuning in Figure \ref{fig:mesh_qual} against the ground truth Blender model.

We run mesh fine-tuning for $n=300$ cycles on all six Shiny Blender scenes with $\lambda_{\mathrm{nrm}}=0.5$. In $\mathcal{T}$, band-limiting is damped umbrella (uniform-Laplacian) diffusion~\citep{TODO-umbrella-smoothing}, and the per-vertex bound caps $\lVert\Delta_j\rVert$ each cycle. Displacements are scaled relative to the disk radius $\sigma$: the vertex learning rate is
$0.1\sigma$, the smoothing width is $2.5\sigma$, and the per-cycle bound is $\delta=0.5\sigma$.
Each geometry step accumulates $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{X}}$ over 8 training views, and each appearance
step takes 40 optimizer steps. In practice, we also condition before the optimizer, Huber-reweighting the pullback~\citep{TODO-huber}
and band-limiting the photometric gradient.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Normal error is measured against the ground-truth Blender normal maps of the 200 Shiny Blender test views per scene. Surface accuracy is measured against the ground-truth Blender models, as the mean point-to-triangle distance from the refined mesh to the camera-visible ground-truth surface, following Ref-NeuS~\citep{refneus}. % TODO: quick metrics explanation (why median normal error)

\textbf{Analysis.}
Quantitatively, mesh fine-tuning is able to refine the extracted mesh and reduce its normal angular error when compared to the ground truth normal across all 6 scenes in ShinyBlender. We report Mean Average Error (MAE) of per-vertex normals and mesh face normals in Table \ref{tab:mesh_normal}. The algorithm also improves surface accuracy on 5/6 scenes (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_normal}). We also assess the effect of any appearance regression of our algorithm as measured by PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPs in Table \ref{tab:mesh_appearance}.

Overall, mesh fine-tuning improves the refined surface (Column 3 of Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_qual}), and it removes the concentric ringing left by marching-cubes extraction on \emph{teapot} and \emph{helmet} (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_ringing}). Mesh fine-tuning targets subtle orientation errors like these, which barely move the surface but tilt its face normals: its updates are restricted to the normal direction, and the normal-consistency term pulls each face toward the smooth, cross-view shading normal. Consistent with this, median face-normal error falls by $61.8\%$ on \emph{helmet} and $53.4\%$ on \emph{teapot} (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_normal}).

Normal error improves on all six scenes, with mean
MAE falling, $6.31^\circ \rightarrow 5.41^\circ$ and yielding ($+14.2\%$) improvement across scenes. 
Accuracy corroborates this on five of six scenes

Crucially, these gains are not paid for in appearance: PSNR improves on five of
six scenes for a mean of $0.45$\,dB (Table~\ref{tab:mesh_appearance}), so the
refinement is correcting geometry rather than trading image quality for it.

In our experiment, we highlight an extension of our representation to conduct mesh refinement \emph{without} altering connectivity. Because barycentric anchoring fixes topology, our proposed mesh refinement method can \emph{adjust} existing surface but \emph{cannot recover} surface that is absent (i.e. large holes). We leave extensions such as coupling the vertex update to remeshing, topology-aware extraction stage, and more detailed comparisons against SOTA mesh refinement methods as future work.
