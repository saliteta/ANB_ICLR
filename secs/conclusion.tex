\section{Conclusion and Discussion}
\vspace{-2mm}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We present automatic neural baking, a representation that composes directly with the traditional
rendering pipeline: an element-wise ``texture'' whose domain is the mesh surface and whose blending
is order-independent, so the asset needs no UV atlas and no depth sort and is evaluated per fragment
at real-time rates. A 2D Gaussian mixture anchored in barycentric coordinates carries the
view-independent fields, an adaptive hierarchical environment map the incident light, and a FastKAN
decoder the view-dependent term. With this bridge in place, the next steps are engine
integration and element-level operations such as merging or editing disks directly.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.} Our method follows the baking setting: it takes a mesh and posed
images as input, is optimized per scene, and bakes the illumination of the input images, including
static shadows, as a lightmap does. Dynamic shadows from moving lights are outside this setting.
When the illumination is known, the environment maps can be fixed to it during training and swapped
or rotated at render time without retraining (App.~\ref{app:scope}), but a full relighting method is
future work. Within a near-convex part, light from the rest of the object and from nearby
surroundings is treated as distant and position-independent, like an uncorrected reflection probe.
Strongly concave objects are therefore the hardest case: \emph{toaster} has our lowest absolute PSNR,
though we still lead every context method there (Table~\ref{tab:shiny_perscene}). Nearby
surroundings can show parallax error. In large scenes, ANB is meant to be applied per reflective
asset, as specialized shaders are in production pipelines, so convex decomposition scales with the
number of such assets, not with scene size. Real-time evaluation is demonstrated in nvdiffrast and in
a Blender scene, not in a shipped engine shader. Our real-capture evaluation covers three
object-centric scenes; scene-scale captures with strong near-field reflections remain to be tested.
